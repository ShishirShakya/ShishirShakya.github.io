# What do women gain from successful revolutions

## Facts

### Policy hook

Revolutions fundamentally transform institutional landscapes. The question for gender equality is whether the revolutionary pathway—violent versus nonviolent—shapes women's post-revolutionary empowerment differently. Both claim to pursue justice and institutional reform, but violent and nonviolent revolutions may build different post-revolutionary institutions with divergent implications for women's rights.

### Main finding

Nonviolent revolutions with explicit regime change intentions consistently yield positive and statistically significant improvements across multiple women's empowerment dimensions—particularly political empowerment, civil liberties, and civil society participation. Violent revolutions demonstrate more ambiguous and less statistically robust effects even after controlling for regime change intention, suggesting that the method of transformation (violent vs. nonviolent) significantly predicts women's post-revolutionary gains.

### Data and setting

Nonviolent and Violent Campaigns and Outcomes (NAVCO) dataset on successful revolutions; Varieties of Democracy dataset for gender-specific metrics; 1950–2019; multiple countries globally. Outcome measures include women's political participation, economic participation, educational attainment, health and survival, and power distribution across multiple indices.

### Research design (plain language)

Fixed-effect difference-in-differences and Callaway Sant'Anna (2021) methodology applied to staggered treatment adoption in heterogeneous contexts. Event study analyses examining effects over time post-revolution. Separate analyses for violent and nonviolent revolutions with explicit regime change intentions as the treatment variable.

### One caveat

Effectiveness significantly mediated by context-specific factors, institutional barriers, and intersectional dynamics. In contexts with entrenched patriarchal norms or specific party structures, numerical presence of women does not guarantee influence on policy or institutional design. Post-revolutionary institutional capacity, organized feminist movements, and international pressure all interact with revolutionary pathway to determine actual women's empowerment outcomes.

## Why it matters

Women's movements often face a strategic choice: work within existing institutions (reform) or support revolutionary change. This paper shows that nonviolent revolutions more reliably translate into women's institutional gains than violent revolutions. For women's rights advocates, the implication is that revolutionary strategy matters—the same goal (regime change) pursued through different means produces different gender outcomes. The stronger results for nonviolent paths may reflect institutional design (nonviolent movements may embed inclusive principles more deeply) or coalition requirements (nonviolent movements require broader social participation, including women). For international donors and multilateral institutions supporting post-conflict reconstruction or democratic transitions, the finding suggests prioritizing support for gender-inclusive institution-building in nonviolent transition contexts. The work also complicates narratives about violence as liberation—revolutionary violence does not automatically liberate women even when it topples authoritarian regimes.
