# Opening the Gate: Navigating Systems Without a Map

## The Uneven Map

The world advances through a complex interplay of intentionality, agency, context, and, at times, randomness. While access and privilege undoubtedly shape opportunities, other factors, such as individual effort, chance, and evolving societal norms, also play significant roles in determining outcomes. For example, research from the Pew Charitable Trusts and Raj Chetty's studies on social mobility demonstrate that both structural factors and individual choices contribute to opportunity, and that upward mobility is possible, though unevenly distributed.

Like many, I wasn't handed a map. In the dim glow of my small study, I'd sift through old books and scattered notes, piecing together fragments wherever I could. Every scribble kept me moving forward. I walked with intention, quietly preparing in the margins. However, there was also luck; a random conversation with a stranger in a random location opened a new door. The months of preparation allowed me to seize the moment. For every success, though, countless others face invisible ceilings despite effort and readiness. Success stories can inspire, but they risk creating survivorship bias if used as the primary evidence of fairness or opportunity.

This essay, based on personal memoir, is for anyone navigating systems (policy leaders, educators, young professionals, and anyone who has ever felt the rules were unwritten or the gate was locked). From the uneven map, I learned not just to walk, but how to navigate, and navigation requires principles, the principles shapes everything that follows.

Many of us are not taught how to navigate the unwritten rules that influence opportunity, sometimes due to systemic barriers, sometimes due to circumstance, and sometimes simply by chance. These barriers are not distributed evenly, and systemic exclusion persists even when effort and preparation are present.

I recognize that not everyone starts with the same resources or opportunities, and that's why my unfinished map, while shaped by my own context and limitations, may still help someone else begin their path or inspire them to chart their own. At the same time, there are those who have leveraged available structures to achieve upward mobility, illustrating the diversity of possible trajectories even within imperfect systems.

## Principles of Navigation

I am an economist by training, a teacher by nature, and a storyteller by necessity. I believe knowledge is most valuable when it empowers rather than excludes, but I also recognize the need for standards, rigor, and sometimes selectivity to ensure quality, safety, and coherence.

Chesterton's Fence is one of my guiding principles: before challenging or changing a system, I work to understand the logic that built it. For example, before revising a curriculum, I interview those who created it to understand their intent and the constraints they faced. Change should be principled rather than merely symbolic. Principled change is guided by clear criteria: it weighs evidence, considers the perspectives of both beneficiaries and those defending the status quo, and seeks to mitigate harm while pursuing improvement.

I want to learn and teach what makes life worth living, how people flourish, think freely, and seek truth. While I value autonomy and decentralization, I also recognize the importance of structure, shared norms, and collective wisdom for progress. However, history and recent experience show that excessive openness, without sufficient structure, can lead to negative outcomes, such as the proliferation of misinformation on open online platforms or the erosion of educational standards through unchecked credential inflation. I hope that the paths I've walked stay open for others, or inspire them to redraw new ones. I still stumble, revise, and unlearn. Even an imperfect map can help someone take a clearer first step.

I am seeking visibility, not for self-promotion, but to amplify alignment and promote unity. I want to be seen without compromising my values. I want to tell stories, not louder ones, but truer ones, that awaken curiosity and clarify meaning.

For every abstract principle, I strive to illustrate it with a concrete example. When I refer to "stochastic tinkering" characterized by high surface area, low downside, and openness to serendipity, I look for experiments where failure won't compromise livelihoods or erode trust. For instance, before rolling out a new course, I pilot it with a handful of volunteers.

I'm learning to go deeper, not wider, but I don't have an ecosystem that naturally supports depth. My strategy has been adaptive: I cast a wide net, maximize surface area, offer value, kindness, and optionality, and stay ready for when the big opportunity arises.

Honoring complexity means acknowledging trade-offs, the limitations of one's own perspective, and the possibility of unintended consequences.

## The Gate and the Game

In the above navigation principle, there is pain in being a false negative, overlooked despite merit. Capable, prepared, but still invisible in elite spaces. Systems for recognizing merit must also prevent false positives (recognizing individuals without readiness). Balancing these errors is a complex challenge, with real stakes for both individuals and society. Academic peer review and professional licensure are forms of necessary gatekeeping that protect quality and safety, even as they risk overlooking unconventional talent. This is not merely an individual burden but a collective moral responsibility. It's about how we, as a society, decide who gets noticed and who doesn't, who gets the chance to contribute and who remains unseen. Both types of error, false positives and negatives, shape lives and institutions. The "gate" and "game" metaphors help us see the stakes clearly.

I've lived the tension of not knowing what to say no to, of being productive but scattered, of trying to be discoverable to many, and in doing so, weakening my gravitational pull for the right few. Still, I hold this tension carefully. Because I know the cost of a false positive (recognized without readiness) in decision-making spaces can be even greater. The consequences of false positives are illustrated in well-known cases, such as failed leadership appointments where unprepared or unethical leaders have caused harm to organizations and the public, or policy errors stemming from the premature promotion of ideas that have not yet been tested or proven. Their choices ripple outward, often at the expense of those who never got the chance.

## The Path Forward

My direction is to play the game with clarity. I aim to master the gate, not to guard it, but to open it and leave it open. My goal is to build what I wish existed, to tell stories that bring others in, and to stay principled, even when the rewards are slow to come.

Systems don't just emerge; they persist, not always because they work, but sometimes because of inertia, coordination problems, or because someone benefits from the friction. For example, the QWERTY keyboard layout persists not because it is optimal, but due to coordination and switching costs. Part of principled change is understanding incentives and trade-offs, not just the stated ideals. I recognize that not all change is desirable or feasible, and that humility and ongoing dialogue, across different cultures, histories, and organizational contexts, are essential for real progress and reassessing assumptions as new evidence emerges.

Because the door I open isn't just for me, it's for the next person who arrives without a map. If you remember only one thing, let it be this: principled change means opening the door and questioning the map, so that those who follow can go farther than we ever imagined.
