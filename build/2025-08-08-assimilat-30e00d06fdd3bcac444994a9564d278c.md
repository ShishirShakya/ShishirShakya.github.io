# Assimilation Reframed: Civic Values, Social Benefits, and the Future of American Belonging

## Introduction

▶ Assimilation in the U.S. has always been a fractured and contested process, shaped by changing power dynamics, exclusionary policies, and the actions of immigrants themselves, both legal and illegal, rather than a straightforward journey toward belonging. Immigrant experiences are also shaped by intersecting factors such as nationality, gender, age, sexuality, and ability, which can create additional barriers or avenues for civic assimilation. Assimilation is not a one-way street. While immigrants are often expected to adapt to American civic life, American society itself is continually reshaped by the languages, foods, beliefs, and customs of newcomers. This reciprocal process of cultural exchange has transformed what it means to be American, fostering a richer, more dynamic national identity.

In this essay, I distinguish between legal immigrants (those who enter or remain in the country with official authorization) and undocumented immigrants (those without legal status), as their experiences and opportunities for assimilation often diverge significantly. I examine how national ideals intersect with exclusionary practices, and how immigrants search for opportunities, yet may encounter or express ambivalence or resistance toward assimilation, sometimes due to personal, cultural, or structural factors. This dynamic reflects an ongoing negotiation between the preservation of cultural identity, the desire for civic inclusion, and the challenges posed by societal barriers. The result is a mutual uncertainty: America, unsure whether it is being loved or merely used; immigrants, unsure whether they are being welcomed or merely tolerated, resulting in assimilation as an ongoing negotiation between civic inclusion, preservation of cultural traditions, and the ongoing challenge of structural barriers.

Let's clarify three key concepts frequently discussed in debates about immigrant incorporation: assimilation, integration, and multiculturalism. Assimilation refers to the process by which immigrants adopt the cultural norms of the host society, often at the expense of their own traditions. Integration describes a two-way process in which both immigrants and the host society adapt, allowing for cultural diversity within a shared civic framework. Multiculturalism, meanwhile, is a perspective or policy that values and maintains distinct cultural identities within a unified society, encouraging groups to retain their traditions while participating in public life. In practice, many immigrants navigate elements of both cultural and civic assimilation, and some may choose to maintain distinct identities over generations. In addition, for the second and third generations, the process of assimilation often takes new forms: children of immigrants may face unique challenges, such as identity conflict, discrimination, or even downward mobility, which complicates the picture of linear progress sometimes assumed in assimilation debates.

However, in this essay, I propose a shift in understanding: rather than expecting assimilation to cultural uniformity, it should be reframed as a process of civic assimilation. In this model, the primary expectation is that all members of society, including immigrants and natives, commit to shared civic values and active participation, rather than abandoning their cultural identities. Concrete indicators of civic assimilation could include metrics such as voting rates, English proficiency, and levels of institutional trust. These metrics provide a measurable framework for tracking the success of civic assimilation over time. Importantly, there are behaviors, customs, or values that may be entirely appropriate in an immigrant's country of origin but can conflict with civic expectations or legal requirements in the United States. For example, deference to authority or traditional gender roles may be valued elsewhere, whereas U.S. civic norms emphasize participatory democracy, gender equality, and transparency. Navigating these differences is central to successful integration and may require targeted orientation, legal education, and community dialogue for both newcomers and receiving communities.

Successful civic assimilation sometimes calls for difficult choices and acts of courage, not only from immigrants, but from all members of society. For immigrants, this may mean reflecting critically on certain customs or practices that, while accepted in their countries of origin, conflict with American civic values or legal standards, such as gender equality, nonviolence, or transparency. Letting go of such practices can be challenging, but it is essential for building trust and mutual respect in a pluralistic society. At the same time, American communities should be open to learning from the positive aspects of immigrant cultures, fostering a two-way process of adaptation and growth. In this way, assimilation becomes not a loss, but a shared project of building a more just and inclusive society.

However, civic assimilation is only one possible model among many, and other countries may prioritize multiculturalism or other frameworks in their approach to integration. While civic assimilation emphasizes shared values and participation, I also recognize that there is no universal formula for integration. There is ongoing scholarly and policy debate about the relative merits of assimilation, integration, and multiculturalism, and no approach is universally successful. Many countries, such as Canada and Australia, have embraced multiculturalism as a guiding principle, actively encouraging the preservation of diverse cultural identities alongside civic engagement. Others employ hybrid or evolving frameworks that reflect their own histories and social dynamics. Ultimately, each society must determine the approach that best aligns with its values, needs, and vision for the future, striking a balance between unity and respect for diversity, while fostering a sense of belonging for all.

## Exclusion and Ambivalence in American History

While the United States upholds the ideal of being a "nation of immigrants," it has also enacted policies and normalized attitudes that target and exclude particular groups. Historic examples, such as the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the internment of Japanese Americans during World War II, illustrate the imposition of legal and social barriers based on selective frameworks. Early laws, such as the Naturalization Act of 1790, restricted citizenship to "free white persons," excluding many from the benefits of citizenship. In these historical contexts, anti-immigrant policies were often applied broadly to all immigrants, regardless of legal status, although later laws increasingly differentiated between legal and illegal immigrants.

Anti-immigrant sentiment intensifies during periods of economic insecurity, as seen during the Great Depression and the early 1980s recession. Cultural and religious boundary-drawing has historically targeted various groups, while legal frameworks and security fears have justified collective punishment in times of crisis. Political scapegoating has recurred, as in the passage of restrictive acts during unrest and the use of anti-immigrant rhetoric in recent political campaigns. Notably, anti-immigrant policies have often blurred the distinction between legal and illegal immigrants, with both groups sometimes facing exclusion and suspicion regardless of their legal status.

Anti-immigrant attitudes have changed over time, but they continue to create a pattern where the idea of an inclusive America exists alongside exclusionary practices. The "nation of immigrants" story has helped many people find opportunities in education, work, and civic life. Still, these opportunities are often limited by policies and attitudes that restrict access, hinder integration, or require individuals to conform to narrow notions of what it means to be American. Because of this, integration in the U.S. has always been a complex process, shaped by both the hope of inclusion and the reality of ongoing barriers.

## Historical and Social Complexity in Assimilation Trajectories

Assimilation in the U.S. has historically been shaped by four overlapping forces: type of migration (economic vs. humanitarian vs. forced), racialization, religion, and policy design. Early European immigrants, such as Irish, Italians, and Jews, were once viewed as unassimilable, yet many were eventually incorporated into whiteness. Their path was aided by legal status, Christian background, industrial jobs, and the ability to distance themselves from stigmatized traits. By contrast, African Americans and Indigenous peoples, who were colonized or forcibly displaced, not immigrants, were excluded from the American social contract entirely. Yet, their resistance and cultural survival have profoundly redefined the meaning of American identity. The 1965 Hart-Celler Act serves as a vivid illustration of how policy design influences the assimilation trajectories of immigrants. By abolishing the national origins quota system, this legislation allowed increased immigration from non-European countries, reshaping racial hierarchies and offering new opportunities for diverse communities to integrate into American society. Still, it is necessary to acknowledge that receiving societies are not always open or adaptable; discrimination, racism, and exclusion remain significant barriers to integration, regardless of policy design.

Anti-immigrant sentiment in the United States has persisted over time, targeting different groups while maintaining similar exclusionary logics. Chinese laborers, Irish and German Catholics, Southern and Eastern Europeans, Japanese Americans, Latinx migrants, and Muslim immigrants have each faced discrimination under shifting rationales such as religion, race, or ideology. Despite changes in targets and methods, conditional belonging remains a core feature.

Yet assimilation has never been uniform, even within these groups. Class, gender, generation, and geography all play a role. Some Italian immigrants, for example, became white faster through intermarriage and military service, while others faced long-term marginalization. Vietnamese and Cuban refugees were welcomed for Cold War purposes, but internal diversity shaped outcomes. Muslim Americans, often framed monolithically, include professionals, refugees, youth, and activists with vastly different trajectories. The gendered dynamics of assimilation are rarely addressed but significant: women often assimilate linguistically and educationally faster but face cultural expectations that constrain full civic participation.

Another deeper blind spot in U.S. discourse is the tension within immigrant communities. Assimilation is not only shaped by the expectations of the host society but also by intra-group stratification within the immigrant group. Authorized economic migrants (e.g., H-1B workers) may resent refugees or asylum seekers who receive quicker legal protections. Undocumented workers may embrace American values yet remain excluded from legal recognition. Meanwhile, elite migrants sometimes distance themselves from poorer co-ethnics, reinforcing class-based divisions. Religion further complicates this landscape: mosques, churches, and temples are not just identity markers; they are sites of mutual aid, civic training, and grassroots assimilation.

The idea that people are either assimilated or "not assimilated" overlooks the more nuanced reality. Many immigrants participate in civic life while preserving their cultural traditions. Some follow mainstream norms for practical reasons but still feel unsure about a society that leaves them out. For some, assimilation is not something they desire or can achieve; for others, it is a necessary means of getting by. Rather than seeing assimilation as the final goal, we should ask what kind of assimilation we expect, who decides the rules, and what might be lost in the process.

Moreover, the current structure of immigration law fosters horizontal resentment. Legal timelines, visa caps, and refugee designations create perceived hierarchies of worthiness. These policies not only produce inequality between immigrants and citizens but also fracture solidarity among immigrants themselves. The result is a fragmented civic landscape, where trust erodes not only between communities but within them.

Today, the concept of America as a melting pot may seem outdated. The country appears more like a mosaic of diverse groups, ethnic, racial, religious, and ideological, that often coexist side by side without fully blending. This kind of coexistence is not necessarily bad, but it calls for new ways of building community. We need civic frameworks that welcome diversity without forcing people to abandon their cultures, and that help individuals find common ground despite their differences.

## Immigrant Agency and Partial Assimilation

Meanwhile, immigrant experiences are not uniform; many actively contribute to shaping American society, building communities, and driving cultural and economic innovation. For instance, the success of Cuban and Vietnamese refugee communities in establishing thriving businesses and revitalizing neighborhoods demonstrates the powerful role of immigrant agency.

However, for some, even when pathways to integration are open, they still choose to maintain strong ties to their heritage for cultural, emotional, or practical reasons, while others are constrained by structural exclusion. This partial assimilation can thus be both a deliberate choice and a consequence of structural barriers. The result is a complex dynamic of conditional inclusion and selective adaptation, one that keeps the nation in a constant state of cultural hybridity and ongoing transformation.

For every obstacle, there are also stories of immigrants taking action, building alliances, and adapting together, all of which add to the American story. Assimilation should be understood primarily as a process of civic assimilation, where the expectation is shared civic values and participation, rather than cultural sameness. Contrary to some stereotypes, most immigrants, even first-generation arrivals, do not inherently lack American civic values. Many are drawn to the United States because of its ideals of freedom, opportunity, and equality. Legal immigrants, whether arriving for economic opportunity or through non-economic pathways such as refuge, asylum, or family reunification, are called to make purposeful decisions about their civic duties. These acts of civic engagement are responsibilities undertaken voluntarily as part of the integration process, not obligations imposed by enforcement. This includes learning English, understanding American institutions, seeking employment or education, and participating in community and civic life as circumstances allow. While first-generation legal immigrants may initially face barriers such as language or unfamiliarity with U.S. systems, studies show that over time, many become actively engaged in civic life, though others encounter persistent difficulties despite significant effort and support. To illustrate, consider the differing experiences of Somali and Colombian newcomers. Somali immigrants often face labor market segmentation that restricts their employment opportunities and limits their civic participation, not due to a lack of motivation but as a consequence of structural barriers such as language barriers and limited recognition of credentials. Conversely, Colombian immigrants may find more opportunities in sectors that align with their skills but still encounter obstacles like discrimination. These contrasts underscore that gaps in participation are usually due to structural challenges rather than a lack of commitment to civic values. (See earlier discussion for specific examples.)

It is essential to acknowledge that while numerous studies emphasize the positive outcomes of civic engagement, some research also documents challenges and setbacks, such as persistent barriers to employment or experiences of discrimination that can hinder integration efforts (see, e.g., Bloemraad, 2013; Waters & Pineau, 2015). Additionally, some groups may resist assimilation for religious, ideological, or political reasons, and for some immigrants, full civic integration remains elusive even after many years in the United States. Recognizing these limitations ensures a more balanced understanding of the assimilation process.

## Social Benefits, the Benefit Cliff, and Dependency

A critical issue in the assimilation debate is the utilization of U.S. social benefits by immigrants, especially non-economic immigrants such as refugees and asylum seekers. While it is desirable to prioritize immigrants who are prepared and motivated for civic assimilation, the realities of American humanitarian commitments often require a careful balancing act. Humanitarian efforts, such as admitting refugees and asylum seekers fleeing crisis, sometimes involve welcoming individuals who may face significant barriers to immediate assimilation, including trauma, language deficits, or limited familiarity with democratic institutions.

Empirical research has shown that immigrants and refugees from countries with dramatically different civic, legal, or cultural traditions often face greater challenges in adapting to American civic life, particularly when they are resettled in the U.S. as a humanitarian act rather than by choice. For instance, refugees from societies with limited exposure to democratic norms, gender equality, or civil rights may require more intensive orientation and support to navigate new expectations around civic participation, legal compliance, and social integration. These challenges are not a reflection of individual intent, but the product of differing socialization and experience, compounded by the dislocation and trauma that humanitarian migrants frequently endure (see, for example, Waters & Pineau, 2015; U.S. Office of Refugee Resettlement reports).

The tradeoff, therefore, lies between the aspiration to select those best positioned for rapid civic integration and the ethical obligation to offer refuge to the vulnerable. Effective policy must acknowledge this tension by pairing humanitarian admissions with robust, well-resourced integration programs that support newcomers' paths toward civic participation, ensuring that humanitarian values and assimilation goals are advanced together.

Legal immigrants, including refugees and asylum seekers with authorized status, are eligible for certain benefits, whereas illegal or undocumented immigrants are generally excluded from most federally funded social programs. Although most legal immigrants contribute significantly to the American economy over time, some, particularly those arriving without immediate economic prospects, rely more heavily on public assistance upon arrival. However, refugees and asylum seekers are far from a homogenous group: their backgrounds, education, and resources vary widely, shaping both their needs and their capacity for self-reliance. This initial dependence is often a result of barriers such as limited language skills, a lack of recognized credentials, the absence of established networks, and unfamiliarity with the U.S. institutional landscape. Many refugees and asylum seekers arrive not knowing what kind of institutions they are being sorted into or how everyday systems in the United States function, which can make navigating and achieving self-sufficiency all the more challenging.

One major challenge is the so-called "benefit cliff," which is the abrupt loss of essential public benefits when an immigrant family's income rises just above eligibility thresholds. Instead of serving as a true launching pad toward economic independence, the structure of many social programs can unintentionally trap recipients in cycles of dependency. Faced with the prospect of losing healthcare, food assistance, or housing support by accepting a modest pay increase or new job, many find themselves unable to move forward. In these cases, American generosity, though well-intentioned, risks being misapplied: social benefits become more like a sand trap than a springboard, impeding upward mobility and long-term integration.

To address this, social benefit policies should be reformed to encourage gradual self-sufficiency. Solutions include phasing out benefits more slowly as income rises, investing in job training and language programs, and providing incentives for employment and skill development. By restructuring assistance to truly empower newcomers, the U.S. can ensure that its tradition of generosity supports, not stifles, immigrant ambition and integration.

However, America's generosity, while admirable, risks being misapplied if it inadvertently fosters dependency rather than empowerment. Most legal immigrants use social benefits only temporarily, and long-term dependency is relatively rare. Most undocumented immigrants are not eligible for federally funded social programs, though some may access state or emergency services. Many immigrants, particularly in today's globalized world, maintain strong transnational ties to their countries of origin, which can shape their civic engagement and sense of belonging in the United States. Both American society and immigrants have vital roles to play in addressing this challenge. Policymakers and institutions must design social programs that truly serve as springboards to independence, structuring benefits to phase out gradually, investing in skills development, and encouraging full participation in economic and civic life. Immigrants, for their part, should approach these opportunities with a commitment to self-improvement and civic engagement: learning English, seeking education and employment, and striving for self-sufficiency as circumstances allow. When both sides actively embrace these responsibilities, American generosity fulfills its highest purpose, empowering newcomers to become engaged, self-reliant members of society and strengthening the nation's civic fabric for future generations.

This dynamic is especially pronounced among refugees, who often face lower initial rates of civic engagement, not because they lack civic values, but due to unique barriers such as trauma, language difficulties, legal hurdles, and economic instability. While non-economic legal immigrants often face additional barriers such as trauma, disrupted education, or unfamiliarity with U.S. systems, the expectation of civic engagement remains important. These are responsibilities that should be embraced as acts of personal commitment to integration, not as mandates enforced by law. With support, purposeful efforts toward integration help accelerate their assimilation and foster a sense of belonging. Refugees may not be eligible to vote or participate fully in elections until they achieve citizenship, a process that can take several years. Undocumented immigrants, by contrast, are typically excluded from voting and many forms of civic participation regardless of their time in the country. Despite these obstacles, many refugees express a strong desire to contribute to their new communities, and research shows that, given time and support, their civic participation rises significantly. For example, a 2021 Pew Research Center survey found that 61% of immigrants who arrived as refugees participated in at least one civic activity (such as volunteering, attending local meetings, or helping neighbors) within their first five years in the U.S. Over generations, refugee communities often become deeply involved in local and national civic life, further enriching the democratic fabric of the United States. In this way, the "nation of immigrants" idea is not about erasing differences, but about bringing people together under shared principles. (See previous section for more on civic participation programs.)

## Moral Hazard and the Integrity of American Generosity

A further challenge in the immigration debate is the risk of "moral hazard," the possibility that the availability of generous social benefits and institutional protections may unintentionally encourage some individuals to migrate to the U.S. primarily to access these resources, rather than to participate in the country's civic or economic life. However, extensive research finds that most immigrants, both legal and illegal, are motivated by economic opportunity, safety, or family reunification, and large-scale abuse of benefits ("welfare migration") is rare. This risk is most commonly raised in the context of undocumented (illegal) immigrants and certain non-economic immigrants, such as those seeking asylum under questionable circumstances.

Most immigrants are motivated by economic opportunity, safety, or family reunification, but there is also potential for abuse. If social programs are perceived abroad as easily accessible or poorly regulated, they can become a pull factor for those seeking benefits rather than contributing to society. This strains public resources, undermines public confidence in the immigration system, and can provoke backlash that ultimately harms all immigrants, regardless of their intentions.

Addressing this issue requires a careful balance: maintaining the nation's humanitarian commitments while ensuring that benefits and protections are reserved for those with genuine need and a willingness to contribute to American civic life. Empirical research indicates that less than 2% of recent arrivals cite access to benefits as a primary motive for migrating to the U.S. This suggests that concerns about "welfare migration" may be overstated, emphasizing the importance of informed policy decisions. Policy responses might include rigorous eligibility verification, cooperation with international partners to dispel myths about "easy" access to benefits, and targeted outreach to ensure that protections remain a safety net, not an incentive for migration.

Ultimately, preserving the integrity of American generosity means designing systems that help the vulnerable and reward those who seek not just shelter, but a place in the nation's shared civic project. Both immigrants and institutions must uphold this standard, immigrants by seeking opportunity and engagement, and American society by safeguarding the purpose and sustainability of its social programs.

## Deportation and Its Impact on Assimilation

Another aspect of the American immigration system is the policy of deportation. While deportation is typically justified as a tool for upholding the rule of law and protecting national security, its application can have far-reaching consequences for both individuals and communities, especially when it targets those who are otherwise contributing to society.

For immigrants, particularly those who have lived in the U.S. for many years, established families, or are integrated into local communities, the threat or reality of deportation can undermine the very process of civic assimilation. Fear of removal discourages participation in civic life, reduces trust in institutions, and can lead to the separation of families, including U.S.-born children. Even lawful permanent residents and refugees may face deportation for minor legal infractions, creating a climate of uncertainty and instability.

From the perspective of American society, an overreliance on deportation as a policy tool risks sending a conflicting message: while the U.S. promotes shared civic values and integration, it may simultaneously undermine these goals by removing individuals who are striving to meet them. Deportation can disrupt communities, impede economic contributions, and erode confidence in the fairness of the system.

A balanced approach to deportation policy recognizes the need for public safety and legal order, but should also account for humanitarian factors, length of residence, family ties, and evidence of civic engagement. Reform efforts might include clearer pathways to regularization, more robust due process protections, and opportunities for rehabilitation for those with minor offenses.

Ultimately, a just and effective assimilation model should foster stability and a sense of belonging, rather than perpetuating a perpetual fear of removal. By aligning deportation policy with the nation's civic ideals and commitment to integration, the U.S. can better fulfill its promise as a nation of immigrants.

## Undocumented Immigrants and the Challenge of Assimilation

No discussion of American assimilation and civic values is complete without addressing the complex reality of undocumented (illegal) immigrants, those who enter or remain in the U.S. without legal authorization, as distinct from legal immigrants who follow authorized pathways. Their presence raises urgent questions about the boundaries of belonging, the application of American generosity, and the meaning of civic responsibility. Throughout this essay, the term "immigrant" is used to refer to both legal and illegal immigrants where appropriate, but specific distinctions are made when legal status shapes opportunities, challenges, or policy implications.

Undocumented immigrants often live and work in the U.S. for years, building families, contributing to local economies, and participating in community life. Many adopt American civic values, send their children to local schools, and even pay taxes. However, their lack of legal status creates profound barriers to full civic assimilation. Fear of detection and deportation discourages engagement with institutions, limits access to social benefits, and undermines trust in government. This precarious existence runs counter to the ideals of stability, participation, and mutual adaptation that define successful civic assimilation.

From a policy perspective, simply excluding undocumented immigrants from civic life contradicts America's tradition of inclusion and undermines the nation's economic and social fabric. Yet, unchecked illegal immigration also challenges the rule of law and public trust in the immigration system. Thus, solutions must strike a balance between compassion, practicality, and respect for legal frameworks.

Meaningful assimilation for undocumented immigrants requires clear, humane pathways to regularization, such as earned legalization or DACA-like protections, that encourage participation and investment in American society. Drawing lessons from past experiences, such as the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) of 1986, is crucial. While IRCA offered wage gains and brought many immigrants into a legal framework, it also exposed enforcement gaps that led to challenges in maintaining order and trust in the system. Addressing these gaps in future policy should be a priority to ensure both effective integration and the credibility of the immigration system. At the same time, policies should address border security and the root causes of unauthorized migration to ensure the system remains fair and credible.

Ultimately, the question is not whether undocumented immigrants can fit within America's civic assimilation model, but whether the nation can design policies and institutions that allow all residents, regardless of legal status, to contribute, belong, and share in the responsibilities and benefits of American life.

## Civic Renewal

To build a more inclusive and cohesive society, concrete steps must be taken across policy, institutions, and civic culture. However, the effectiveness of such efforts can be constrained by political polarization, resource limits, and social resistance. For example, between 2010 and 2022, naturalization rates among eligible immigrants in the U.S. increased from 56% to 64%, reflecting ongoing civic engagement and adaptation. During the COVID-19 pandemic, immigrants, especially in healthcare and essential services, played a crucial role, further demonstrating the value of integration over forced assimilation. Meanwhile, contemporary debates over DACA, refugee admissions, and state-level sanctuary laws show how the struggle over assimilation and integration continues to shape policy and public opinion.

First, immigration policy should expand and streamline pathways to citizenship for undocumented and long-term residents, while strengthening anti-discrimination protections in housing, employment, and education. Equally important is investment in healthcare, legal aid, and language access to ensure immigrant communities can fully participate in public life. Second, institutions must cultivate civic infrastructure by promoting inclusive school curricula that reflect the histories of immigrants, supporting immigrant-led organizations and leadership development, and embedding cross-cultural programs in schools, workplaces, and community spaces.

Third, immigrants themselves have a vital role to play by engaging in naturalization, voting, and local governance, opportunities generally limited to those with legal status; learning English while preserving their heritage languages; and building relationships across cultural lines to foster mutual trust and understanding. Purposeful civic engagement by all legal immigrants, regardless of their initial motivation for migration, is essential for successful assimilation. Such engagement is an act of responsibility freely chosen by individuals, not a requirement imposed by authorities. By actively participating in American civic life, immigrants not only support their own integration but also strengthen the pluralistic and democratic character of the United States. (For a summary of previous arguments on civic duties and integration, see earlier discussion.) Fourth, media institutions must support narrative change through media literacy initiatives that combat disinformation and amplify the voices of immigrants in journalism, the arts, and storytelling. Ultimately, the United States can learn from global models, such as multilingual public services, anti-xenophobia campaigns, and community sponsorship programs, that promote integration without requiring cultural erasure. For example, Canada's point-based immigration system pairs labor market needs with robust integration support, while Germany has invested heavily in language and job training programs for newcomers, and Australia regularly reviews its skilled migration targets to match economic trends. These approaches highlight both the promise and the complexity of designing policies that address diverse social and economic goals. Together, these efforts shift assimilation from a one-sided burden into a shared civic project rooted in dignity, pluralism, and democratic participation.

Assimilation should not be about proving cultural purity or forcing everyone to be the same. It should be a process where people and institutions adapt to one another, based on shared responsibility and mutual respect.

The American economy also has a longstanding reliance on the labor and contributions of both legal and illegal immigrants, who fill essential roles in sectors such as agriculture, construction, healthcare, and technology. This reality creates a complex dynamic: while economic necessity drives demand for immigrant labor, public debate often centers on concerns about legality, integration, and social cohesion. For example, the story of José, an undocumented agricultural worker from Guatemala, illustrates how such workers often become deeply rooted in local communities, supporting both their families and the broader economy despite lacking legal status (based on a composite of cases reported by the New York Times, 2023). Programs like the TN visa offer a legal and streamlined pathway for skilled professionals from Canada and Mexico to work temporarily in the United States, helping to meet labor market needs while reducing unauthorized migration. Similarly, TPS provides a humanitarian solution for individuals unable to return home due to a crisis, allowing them to contribute openly and legally to the U.S. economy for as long as their status is valid.

By expanding and effectively managing such legal channels, the United States can reduce the resentment sometimes directed at undocumented immigrants, offering clearer, regulated alternatives for both migrants and employers. However, these programs are explicitly temporary: TN and TPS holders are expected to return to their countries of origin when their status expires. While this clarity may help align economic goals and address concerns about long-term unauthorized presence, it raises important humanitarian questions. Many temporary workers and TPS recipients build strong ties in the U.S., and requiring them to leave after years of legal residence can disrupt families, communities, and economic stability. Thus, a humane approach must balance economic needs, lawful pathways, and the reality of human attachment, potentially by offering adjustment options for those who have established deep roots over time.

If American generosity is to fulfill its highest purpose, both society and newcomers must embrace their roles: Americans by fostering opportunity and policies that empower, and immigrants by striving for self-reliance and active participation. When both immigrants and institutions work together with courage, accountability, and empathy, belonging is not something to win, but something everyone can help create.

A true "win-win" scenario is possible when immigration policy combines lawful, flexible pathways, meaningful integration support, clear expectations, and humane adjustment options. By expanding and streamlining legal channels for both high-skill and essential low-skill workers, and pairing these with targeted humanitarian admissions, the United States can meet its labor needs, reduce unauthorized migration and the resentment it can breed, and maintain its humanitarian ideals. To avoid creating a moral hazard, eligibility criteria and time-limited benefits should be transparent, with support tied to integration benchmarks, such as language acquisition and employment. For those who develop long-term ties and contribute positively, fair adjustment options can offer security and stability, while enforcement against unauthorized hiring and exploitation reassures the public that the system is fair. In this way, policy can promote economic vitality, uphold humanitarian values, and minimize the risk of system abuse, ensuring that both newcomers and the broader society benefit.

Looking beyond the U.S., global migration trends continue to evolve, and debates over assimilation, integration, and multiculturalism are ongoing in many societies. Countries adopt different models to suit their histories, demographics, and civic traditions, and the search for effective, inclusive frameworks remains a vital and dynamic challenge worldwide.

However, it must be acknowledged that civic assimilation is not fully feasible for those who enter and remain in the country illegally. Without legal status, undocumented immigrants cannot fully participate in civic life, and their lack of recognition makes mutual trust and adaptation with American institutions difficult; thus, many of the pathways this essay proposes for civic assimilation may not apply to them. Furthermore, the creation of clear, humane, and practical pathways to regularization, while necessary for addressing the realities of those already contributing to society, also risks feeding the very moral hazard discussed earlier by inadvertently incentivizing further unlawful entry. It is therefore a civic duty of the nation to reduce incentives for unauthorized immigration through enforcement and transparent, fair systems, so that civic assimilation becomes a meaningful, attainable goal for all.
